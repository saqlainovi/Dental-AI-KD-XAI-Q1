\begin{abstract}
Recent advancements in deep learning offer substantial promise for automated dental image analysis, yet clinical adoption remains hindered by opaque decision-making and a lack of uncertainty estimation. In this study, we propose DentalXAI, a unified framework for tooth segmentation and multi-pathology classification in panoramic radiographs, enhanced with explainable AI (XAI) and uncertainty-aware clinical triage. The segmentation module leverages a ConvNeXt-Tiny encoder with an All-MLP decoder (28.45M parameters), achieving a Dice score of 0.9488 and an Intersection over Union (IoU) of 0.9038 on an internal test set (N=250). On the external TUFTS holdout set (N=968), the model demonstrated robust zero-shot generalization with a remarkable specificity of 0.9828 and a Dice score of 0.6489. For multi-pathology detection, an EfficientNet-B2 classifier achieves a macro Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) of 0.8399 and a macro F1-score of 0.6788, notably yielding a perfect recall (1.0000) for dental caries detection. To ensure clinical safety, we integrate Monte Carlo (MC) Dropout (20 forward passes) to quantify epistemic uncertainty, yielding a mean variance of 0.0030. At a predefined safety threshold of $\tau = 0.05$, the system safely auto-approved 100\% of test predictions. Explainability is provided via Grad-CAM visualizations. The entire pipeline operates efficiently on a single NVIDIA RTX 3060 (8 GB VRAM), requiring only 2.50 hours for complete training. DentalXAI provides accurate, transparent, and uncertainty-aware diagnostics, bridging the gap toward reliable deployment in routine dental practice. Code is available at \url{https://github.com/saqlainovi/Dental-AI-KD-XAI-Q1}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Oral diseases, including dental caries, periapical lesions, and impacted teeth, remain a major global public health concern \cite{petersen2003world}. Panoramic radiography is a universally utilized diagnostic modality due to its broad field of view, cost-effectiveness, and low radiation exposure adhering to the ALARA principle \cite{farman2005alara, horner2013selection}. Despite its ubiquity, interpreting panoramic radiographs is labor-intensive and subject to inter-observer variability, especially in high-throughput clinical settings \cite{meurer2021panoramic}. 

The advent of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) has spurred significant progress in medical image analysis \cite{esteva2017dermatologist, kermany2018identifying}. In dentistry, automated tooth segmentation and pathology detection have the potential to streamline diagnostic workflows \cite{chen2019tooth}. However, most existing frameworks treat these tasks in isolation and function as "black boxes" lacking interpretability, which inherently limits clinicians' trust \cite{begoli2019need, kelly2019key}. Furthermore, traditional deep learning models provide deterministic point estimates, failing to express confidence or uncertainty in their predictions. This is a critical flaw in medical domains where overconfident erroneous predictions can lead to improper patient management \cite{tjoa2021survey}.

To address these challenges, we introduce \textbf{DentalXAI}, an end-to-end explainable and uncertainty-aware framework. Our system combines accurate segmentation using a modern ConvNeXt architecture~\cite{liu2022convnext} with efficient pathology detection via EfficientNet~\cite{tan2019efficientnet}. Both networks leverage ImageNet-pretrained representations, transferring rich visual knowledge from large-scale general-purpose data to the specialized dental domain---a paradigm related to knowledge distillation~\cite{hinton2015distilling} and transfer learning for medical imaging~\cite{wang2023knowledge, dou2020unpaired}. Crucially, we overlay these predictive models with Monte Carlo (MC) Dropout~\cite{gal2016dropout} for uncertainty estimation and Grad-CAM~\cite{selvaraju2017gradcam} for explainability. The resulting triage system routes high-confidence predictions for automated approval while deferring uncertain cases to human experts, establishing a safe human-in-the-loop paradigm.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Tooth Segmentation in Dental Radiographs}
Accurate tooth isolation is a prerequisite for localized pathology detection. Early approaches relied on thresholding and active contours, which struggled with overlapping structures \cite{nomir2005human}. The introduction of architectures like U-Net \cite{ronneberger2015unet} marked a paradigm shift. Jader et al. \cite{jader2018deep} and Silva et al. \cite{silva2021automatic} successfully applied CNN-based segmentation to panoramic x-rays. Recently, vision transformers (ViTs) and advanced CNNs like ConvNeXt have demonstrated superior feature extraction capabilities \cite{xie2021segformer, liu2021swin}, motivating our choice of a ConvNeXt-Tiny encoder for robust dental structure delineation.

\subsection{Multi-Pathology Detection}
Beyond segmentation, detecting multiple dental pathologies simultaneously is critical. Deep learning models have shown high efficacy in identifying caries~\cite{lee2018caries, casalegno2019caries} and periodontal bone loss~\cite{krois2019deep}. The DENTEX challenge~\cite{hamamci2023dentex} established the first large-scale benchmark for multi-pathology detection in panoramic radiographs. However, dealing with class imbalances---such as the underrepresentation of periapical lesions compared to caries---remains a persistent challenge~\cite{schwendicke2019convolutional}. Recent multi-task architectures aim to classify diverse conditions, but balancing sensitivity and specificity across varying visual manifestations is complex.

\subsection{Explainability in Medical AI}
Trust in clinical AI is contingent on interpretability. Techniques such as Grad-CAM \cite{selvaraju2017gradcam}, LIME \cite{ribeiro2016why}, and SHAP \cite{lundberg2017unified} provide post-hoc explanations by highlighting regions responsible for a model's prediction. In dental AI, visualizing the exact location of a carious lesion or impacted tooth ensures that the model's reasoning aligns with clinical diagnostic criteria \cite{tonekaboni2019clinicians}.

\subsection{Uncertainty Estimation}
Deterministic neural networks often lack the capacity to express ``I don't know''~\cite{blundell2015weight}. Bayesian deep learning offers a mathematically grounded solution, but it is computationally expensive. Monte Carlo (MC) Dropout, introduced by Gal and Ghahramani~\cite{gal2016dropout}, provides a practical approximation of Bayesian inference. By performing multiple forward passes with dropout enabled during inference, one can calculate the epistemic variance, facilitating safe clinical triage by flagging highly uncertain predictions for human review. Leibig et al.~\cite{leibig2017uncertainty} demonstrated the clinical value of uncertainty-aware predictions in ophthalmic imaging, while Nair et al.~\cite{nair2020uncertainty} validated this approach for neurological lesion segmentation, motivating our adoption of MC Dropout for dental triage.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Datasets}
\label{sec:datasets}

Three publicly available datasets were curated for this study (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}):

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{DentalAI}~\cite{white2014oral}: 2,495 panoramic radiographs (original resolution: $4577 \times 3051$) with Supervisely polygon annotations for tooth segmentation. Partitioned into training (N=1,996), validation (N=249), and test (N=250) sets.
    \item \textbf{DENTEX Challenge}~\cite{hamamci2023dentex}: 705 labelled panoramic radiographs ($2850 \times 1316$) with COCO-format bounding box annotations for four pathology classes: Impacted (N\textsubscript{ann}=534), Caries (N\textsubscript{ann}=1,258), Periapical Lesion (N\textsubscript{ann}=240), and Deep Caries (N\textsubscript{ann}=1,497). Partitioned into training (N=547), validation (N=51), and test (N=107).
    \item \textbf{TUFTS Dental Database}~\cite{tufts2020dental}: 1,000 radiographs ($1615 \times 840$) with binary tooth masks, used exclusively as an external holdout for zero-shot cross-domain evaluation (N=968 after excluding images with empty masks).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preprocessing}

All images underwent a standardized preprocessing pipeline:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{CLAHE Enhancement}: Contrast-Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization was applied on the L channel of LAB color space (\texttt{clipLimit}=2.0, tile grid $8 \times 8$) to improve soft-tissue and enamel contrast without amplifying radiographic noise~\cite{pitts2017dental}.
    \item \textbf{Letterbox Resizing}: Images were resized to $512 \times 512$ using aspect-ratio-preserving letterbox padding (black fill), avoiding geometric distortion of dental arches.
    \item \textbf{Annotation Conversion}: Supervisely polygon annotations were rasterized into binary masks; COCO bounding boxes were converted to multi-label image-level vectors.
    \item \textbf{Data Augmentation}: During training, horizontal flipping, random rotation ($\pm 7^{\circ}$), and brightness/contrast jittering were applied via Albumentations~\cite{buslaev2020albumentations} to simulate clinical sensor variability.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Tooth Segmentation Architecture}
The segmentation module utilizes an ImageNet-pretrained \cite{deng2009imagenet} ConvNeXt-Tiny encoder \cite{liu2022convnext}. The encoder produces hierarchical feature maps with channel dimensions of [96, 192, 384, 768]. These features are processed by a lightweight All-MLP decoder \cite{xie2021segformer} with a unified embedding dimension of 256. 
The network was optimized using the AdamW optimizer \cite{loshchilov2019adamw} with a differential learning rate strategy: the encoder utilized $0.5\times$ the base learning rate, while the decoder used $2.0\times$. The base learning rate was set to $1\times10^{-4}$ following a cosine annealing schedule with warm restarts ($T_0=15$, $T_{mult}=2$) \cite{loshchilov2017cosine}. We employed a combined Dice and Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss with equal weighting. Training ran for 60 epochs with a batch size of 4 using Automatic Mixed Precision (AMP) in FP16.

\subsection{Multi-Pathology Classification}
For pathology classification, we leveraged EfficientNet-B2 \cite{tan2019efficientnet}. The classification head was modified to a Global Average Pooling (GAP) layer, followed by Dropout (0.3), a Linear layer (1408 to 512), Batch Normalization, ReLU activation, a second Dropout (0.3), and a final Linear projection to 4 pathology classes.
We utilized a Focal BCE loss ($\gamma=2$) \cite{lin2017focal} with $\alpha$ parameters scaled inversely to class frequencies to mitigate imbalance. Optimization used AdamW (LR $1\times10^{-4}$) with cosine annealing over 40 epochs and a batch size of 16 using AMP FP16. Optimal decision thresholds were calibrated using the Youden index on a validation subset (N=51).

\subsection{Explainability and Uncertainty Estimation}
Grad-CAM was applied to the final convolutional layers of the EfficientNet-B2 classifier to generate heatmaps highlighting predictive pathological regions. For uncertainty estimation, MC Dropout was utilized at test time. We performed $T=20$ stochastic forward passes with dropout probability $p=0.30$. The variance across these passes quantified the epistemic uncertainty.

\section{Experimental Results}

\subsection{Computational Efficiency}
The entire pipeline is highly resource-efficient. The total parameter count is 36.88M (28.45M for segmentation, 8.43M for classification). Training on a single NVIDIA RTX 3060 (8 GB VRAM) required a peak VRAM of 5479 MB. Total execution time was approximately 2.50 hours: 2.30 hours for segmentation (60 epochs, $\sim$137.5s/epoch), 0.18 hours for disease classification (40 epochs, $\sim$15.8s/epoch), and $\sim$39 seconds for MC dropout inference.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Principal Findings}
In this study, we developed and rigorously evaluated DentalXAI, a highly efficient, transparent, and robust computational framework for panoramic dental radiography analysis. The segmentation pipeline, anchored by ConvNeXt-Tiny and an All-MLP decoder, demonstrated state-of-the-art delineation capabilities with a Dice score of 0.9488 on our internal dataset. Simultaneously, the EfficientNet-B2 classification branch delivered a macro AUC of 0.8399 across four distinct clinical pathologies. Crucially, the system exhibited a perfect recall (1.0000) for Caries, a fundamental requirement for a frontline screening tool where missing a decayed tooth is clinically unacceptable. The integration of MC Dropout revealed an remarkably low mean epistemic variance of 0.0030, allowing our automated triage system to safely auto-approve 100\% of the tested samples under a strict safety threshold ($\tau=0.05$).

\subsection{Comparison with Prior Work}
Our findings extend existing paradigms by integrating segmentation, classification, XAI, and uncertainty estimation into a single cohesive pipeline. While prior studies utilizing U-Net~\cite{ronneberger2015unet} and its variants achieve Dice scores ranging from 0.88 to 0.92 on dental segmentation tasks~\cite{silva2021automatic, jader2018deep}, our ConvNeXt-based architecture~\cite{liu2022convnext} attains a superior Dice of 0.9488. The SegFormer architecture~\cite{xie2021segformer} demonstrated that All-MLP decoders can match heavier decoder designs; our adoption of this lightweight decoder with a modernized CNN backbone validates this finding in the dental domain. Furthermore, many contemporary pathology models function as deterministic black boxes~\cite{schwendicke2019convolutional}. DentalXAI's addition of Grad-CAM~\cite{selvaraju2017gradcam} ensures that its internal representation aligns with the physiological location of pathologies, addressing the interpretability bottleneck identified by clinical stakeholders~\cite{begoli2019need, kelly2019key}.

\subsection{Class Imbalance and Periapical Detection}
Despite the strong global performance, the model exhibited variations across specific conditions. The detection of periapical lesions proved challenging, yielding an F1-score of 0.2414, a precision of 0.1591, and a specificity of 60.22\%. This degradation is primarily attributed to severe class imbalance (only 14 positive condition cases vs. 93 negative cases) and the visually subtle, radiolucent nature of apical variations which often blend with surrounding trabecular bone structure. While Focal loss successfully prevented the model from entirely ignoring the minority class (achieving 0.5000 recall), the high rate of false positives (FP=37) suggests that multi-scale structural context is needed for periapical lesions. Deep Caries detection, however, maintained a robust F1-score of 0.7193.

\subsection{Cross-Domain Generalization}
A pivotal stress-test for any clinical AI is external validation. When subjected to the out-of-distribution TUFTS dataset (N=968) in a purely zero-shot manner, the segmentation Dice score understandably dropped from 0.9488 to 0.6489. However, the model retained an exceptional specificity of 0.9828. This high specificity confirms that while the model may under-segment complex anatomical boundary variations found in distinct demographic populations and disparate x-ray machine calibrations, it safely refrains from producing egregious false positive masks (hallucinations).

\subsection{Uncertainty Behavior Analysis}
The extremely low mean epistemic variance (0.0030) implies that the classification manifold learned by EfficientNet-B2 is highly stable for the test distribution. By safely auto-approving 100\% of the cases, the triage module demonstrates that automated pipelines can theoretically handle the bulk of standard radiological screening. In a real-world high-throughput clinic, retaining a dynamic $\tau$ threshold ensures that novel, out-of-distribution artifacts will naturally induce higher variance and thus trigger human-in-the-loop interventions, prioritizing patient safety over absolute automation.

\subsection{Limitations}
Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged transparently:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Single-source segmentation training}: The segmentation model was trained exclusively on DentalAI data. Multi-site training with heterogeneous scanner protocols would improve generalization.
    \item \textbf{2D analysis only}: Panoramic radiographs are inherently 2D projections, and some pathologies (e.g., buccal/lingual caries) may be occluded. Integration with CBCT data~\cite{jacobs2018cbct} is a natural extension.
    \item \textbf{Small validation set for threshold calibration}: Calibrated thresholds were optimized on only 51 validation images, which may not generalize across clinical populations.
    \item \textbf{Periapical lesion detection}: The F1 of 0.2414 for periapical lesions is insufficient for autonomous diagnosis. Targeted augmentation, specialized detectors, or larger annotated datasets with more periapical cases are needed.
    \item \textbf{No prospective clinical validation}: All evaluations are retrospective. Prospective studies with clinical outcome tracking are needed before deployment.
    \item \textbf{MC Dropout expressiveness}: The 100\% auto-approve rate, while indicative of high model confidence on in-distribution data, raises questions about whether dropout alone provides sufficient uncertainty signal. Ensemble methods~\cite{blundell2015weight} or evidential deep learning could offer complementary perspectives.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
We presented DentalXAI, an integrated framework delivering explainable tooth segmentation and multi-pathology detection with uncertainty-aware clinical triage. Leveraging ConvNeXt-Tiny (28.45M parameters) and EfficientNet-B2 (8.43M parameters), the model achieved a Dice score of 0.9488 for tooth segmentation, a macro AUC of 0.8399 for four-pathology classification with perfect caries recall (1.0000), and safe automated triage via MC Dropout (mean variance 0.0030)---all on a single consumer-grade NVIDIA RTX 3060 GPU (8\,GB VRAM) in 2.50 hours of training. By coupling automated screening with Grad-CAM visual explanations and calibrated safety triage, DentalXAI addresses key barriers to the clinical adoption of AI-assisted dental diagnostics.

Future work will focus on (i) multi-site federated data collection to improve cross-domain sensitivity, (ii) CBCT integration for 3D pathology assessment, (iii) prospective clinical trials to validate real-world diagnostic utility, and (iv) extension to additional pathology classes including periodontal disease grading and root fracture detection.
