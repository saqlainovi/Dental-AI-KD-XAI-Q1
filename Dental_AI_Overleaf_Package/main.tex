\begin{abstract}
Translating deep learning into chairside dental screening remains blocked by heavy model footprints, cross-site fragility, uncalibrated confidence, and opaque reasoning. We address all four barriers in a single two-stage pipeline. First, a Swin-T teacher (28.51M parameters) trained on 3,245 tooth masks generates pseudo-labels for 2,032 unannotated DENTEX panoramic radiographs. These labels supervise an ultra-compact TinyUNet student (0.118M parameters, 1.42\,MB on disk) through a composite BCE\,+\,Dice\,+\,KL distillation loss over 50 epochs on a laptop RTX\,3060 (peak 676\,MB VRAM, 3.55 hours total). On the DENTEX internal test set the student reaches a Dice of 0.8588 (IoU 0.7588, AUC 0.9852); on the completely held-out TUFTS benchmark (968 non-empty masks) it scores 0.8886 Dice (IoU 0.8047, AUC 0.9959) with no fine-tuning, confirming cross-domain robustness. Specialist Swin classification heads yield a macro-F1 of 0.7284 over four pathologies, including 1.0000 recall on deep caries. Monte Carlo Dropout ($N{=}10$) epistemic triage auto-approves 82.2\% of cases and routes the remaining 17.8\% for radiologist review. Grad-CAM, LIME, and KernelSHAP deliver tooth-level visual explanations. The student runs at 147.1\,FPS (6.8\,ms latency), making real-time edge deployment practical in resource-limited clinics.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A single panoramic radiograph (OPG) reveals all 32 adult teeth, the mandibular canal, the temporomandibular joints, and the maxillary sinuses in one exposure~\cite{white2014oral}. This makes OPGs the most widely ordered dental image worldwide, requested before nearly every first consultation, orthodontic referral, or pre-surgical assessment~\cite{petersen2003world, farman2005alara}. Yet reading an OPG well is harder than it looks: geometric magnification varies across the arch, ghost shadows from the contralateral side overlap with real anatomy, and patient motion blurs fine radiolucencies~\cite{horner2013selection}. In busy outpatient settings, diagnostic fatigue leads to missed early caries, subtle periapical lesions, and delayed impaction referrals~\cite{meurer2021panoramic}.

Deep learning has matched specialists in several medical imaging tasks~\cite{esteva2017dermatologist, kermany2018identifying}, and dental AI is no exception---Mask R-CNN, Swin Transformers, and Vision Transformers have all shown promise on tooth segmentation and pathology detection~\cite{chen2019tooth, le2022deep, hamamci2023dentex}. Deploying these models in a real dental chair, however, exposes four practical barriers:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Compute cost.} State-of-the-art vision transformers exceed 30\,M parameters and need high-end GPUs that clinics do not own~\cite{sandler2018mobilenetv2}.
    \item \textbf{Domain shift.} Models trained on one institution's X-ray hardware often fail on images from another centre~\cite{zech2018variable}.
    \item \textbf{Overconfidence.} Standard softmax outputs look certain even when the model is wrong, risking silent diagnostic failures~\cite{guo2017calibration, begoli2019need}.
    \item \textbf{Black-box opacity.} Without visual evidence linking a prediction to specific anatomical features, clinicians distrust the output~\cite{kelly2019key}.
\end{enumerate}

We tackle all four problems in a single, end-to-end framework that chains knowledge distillation, specialist disease classifiers, multi-granularity explainability, and Monte Carlo Dropout uncertainty triage---all trainable on a commodity laptop GPU in under four hours.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Clinical Datasets}
Three independent panoramic radiography collections were used (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}, Figure~\ref{fig:class_dist}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{DentalAI} (2,495 images)---tooth polygon masks from multiple PACS archives, combined with TUFTS boundary annotations to give 3,245 segmentation training instances for the teacher.
    \item \textbf{DENTEX} (2,332 images)---the international Dental Enumeration and Diagnosis Challenge~\cite{hamamci2023dentex}: 2,032 training, 50 validation, 107 test radiographs labelled for Caries, Deep Caries, Periapical Lesions, and Impacted Teeth.

\subsection{Preprocessing}
Panoramic radiographs average $3000{\times}2000$ pixels (over 15\,GB uncompressed across the full corpus). We normalise intensities to $[0,1]$, resize to $512{\times}512$ via bilinear interpolation on-the-fly, and pack batches of eight, keeping peak GPU allocation at 676.55\,MB---well within the 6\,GB ceiling of our RTX\,3060.

\subsection{Knowledge Distillation Architecture}
Our two-stage design (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}) pairs a large teacher with a tiny student:

\begin{figure*}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/Fig2_architecture.png}
\caption{End-to-end pipeline. \textbf{Stage~1:} Swin-T teacher generates pseudo-masks. \textbf{Stage~2:} TinyUNet learns from them via composite KD loss. \textbf{Stage~3:} Specialist Swin heads classify four pathologies. \textbf{Stage~4:} Grad-CAM/LIME/SHAP explainability and MC Dropout triage.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure*}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Teacher (Swin-T + UPerNet):} A hierarchical Swin-Tiny backbone with a UPerNet decoder~\cite{liu2021swin}, totalling 28.51\,M parameters. Trained on the 2,245 DentalAI masks plus TUFTS boundary annotations.
    \item \textbf{Student (TinyUNet):} A four-stage encoder--decoder with filter widths $[16, 32, 64, 128]$ and a 256-channel bottleneck, yielding only 118,481 parameters (0.118\,M) and a 1.42\,MB checkpoint.
\end{itemize}

The student learns through a composite distillation loss:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{total} = \alpha\,\mathcal{L}_{BCE}(y_{pred}, y_{pseudo}) + \beta\,\mathcal{L}_{Dice}(y_{pred}, y_{pseudo}) + \gamma\,\mathcal{L}_{KD}(p_S, p_T)
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where the KL-divergence term transfers softened boundary distributions:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{KD} = \tau^2 \cdot D_{KL}\!\bigl(\sigma(z_S/\tau) \;\|\; \sigma(z_T/\tau)\bigr), \quad \tau = 3.0
\end{equation}
with weights $\alpha{=}1$, $\beta{=}1$, $\gamma{=}0.5$. Training ran for 50 epochs with AdamW ($\eta_0{=}10^{-3}$, cosine anneal to $10^{-6}$, batch size 8) on an NVIDIA RTX\,3060 Laptop GPU (6\,GB VRAM, 55\,W TGP). Total wall-clock time: \textbf{3.55 hours} (mean 255.47\,s per epoch, 37.45\,W average power draw, 46.37$^\circ$C average GPU temperature). Figure~\ref{fig:training_time} details per-epoch and cumulative timing; Figure~\ref{fig:gpu_telem} tracks live GPU telemetry.

\subsection{Specialist Diagnostic Heads}
Four Swin-based classification heads, one per pathology, are fine-tuned on localised tooth ROIs. Thresholds are set via weak-recall-balanced optimisation on the validation split:
\begin{equation}
\theta_c^* = \arg\max_{\theta \in [0.1, 0.9]} \bigl[ F1_c(\theta) + \lambda \cdot \text{Recall}_c(\theta) \bigr]
\end{equation}
yielding $\theta_{\text{caries}}{=}0.62$, $\theta_{\text{deep caries}}{=}0.84$, $\theta_{\text{periapical}}{=}0.58$, $\theta_{\text{impacted}}{=}0.78$.

\subsection{Epistemic Uncertainty Estimation}
We quantify predictive uncertainty with Monte Carlo (MC) Dropout~\cite{gal2016dropout}. At test time, $N{=}10$ stochastic forward passes (dropout $p{=}0.20$) produce:
\begin{equation}
\bar{p}(x) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N} \hat{p}_t(x),\quad
\sigma_{\text{ep}}^2(x) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\bigl(\hat{p}_t(x)-\bar{p}(x)\bigr)^2
\end{equation}
A clinical safety threshold $\tau_{\text{safe}}{=}0.05$ partitions predictions: cases with $\sigma_{\text{ep}}^2 \le 0.05$ proceed to automated screening; the rest are routed for radiologist review.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Distillation works for dental imaging.}
A recurring concern in knowledge distillation is whether a student that is two orders of magnitude smaller can preserve clinically relevant spatial detail. Our results show that it can: TinyUNet retains 98.7\% of the teacher's Dice while occupying 1.42\,MB---small enough to ship as a mobile application. The composite BCE\,+\,Dice\,+\,KL loss plays a key role here; removing the KL term in preliminary ablations dropped the TUFTS Dice by 0.03, suggesting that soft boundary distributions carry information that hard pseudo-labels alone do not.

\paragraph{Cross-domain robustness without fine-tuning.}
The fact that the student achieves a \emph{higher} Dice on the TUFTS external holdout (0.8886) than on the DENTEX internal test set (0.8588) deserves attention. We attribute this to the teacher's broader training base, which includes TUFTS boundary annotations. Even so, the student never saw any TUFTS image during its own training; the knowledge transfer is indirect, mediated entirely through pseudo-labels generated on DENTEX. This is encouraging for deployment across clinics with heterogeneous X-ray hardware.

\paragraph{Clinical implications.}
From a clinical safety standpoint, missing a deep carious lesion can escalate to irreversible pulp necrosis or facial cellulitis within days. Our framework's 1.0000 recall on Deep Caries (zero false negatives) directly addresses this risk. Periapical Lesion recall (0.4118) is the weakest link; this is consistent with the inherent difficulty of detecting early periapical rarefying osteitis on 2D projections, where buccal cortical bone often obscures the radiolucency. Rather than masking this limitation, the MC Dropout triage layer flags ambiguous cases for human review, ensuring that low-recall classes do not silently pass through.

\paragraph{Uncertainty triage as a clinical safety net.}
The 82.2\% auto-approval rate means that, in a typical 100-patient screening day, a dentist would need to review only 18 flagged cases instead of all 100. At the same time, the flagged 17.8\% contains most of the model's errors (accuracy drops from 94.3\% in the low-uncertainty stratum to 63.2\% in the high-uncertainty stratum). This ``know-when-you-don't-know'' property is arguably more important than raw accuracy for regulatory acceptance.

\paragraph{Explainability for trust.}
Grad-CAM highlights the coarse region triggering a diagnosis, LIME reveals which superpixel clusters support or oppose it, and KernelSHAP assigns additive feature attributions. Together, they give the clinician three complementary lenses: ``where is the model looking?'' (Grad-CAM), ``which patch matters?'' (LIME), and ``how much does each feature contribute?'' (SHAP). In informal feedback from two practising dentists, the LIME overlay was considered the most intuitive, while the SHAP bar chart was preferred when comparing differential diagnoses.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, panoramic radiography produces a 2D projection of complex 3D anatomy; buccal or lingual bone resorption may be hidden behind dense cortical bone. Extending the pipeline to low-dose 3D CBCT volumes is a natural next step. Second, the periapical lesion class suffers from low prevalence (17 positives in 107 test images) and inherent detection difficulty, leading to an F1 of only 0.5385. Targeted data augmentation, contrastive pre-training on periapical-enriched cohorts, or dedicated high-magnification root-apex attention modules may improve sensitivity. Third, the current MC Dropout threshold ($\tau{=}0.05$) was calibrated on 107 test images; a larger prospective validation on multi-centre clinical data is needed before regulatory submission. Finally, while informal clinician feedback on the XAI visualisations was positive, a formal user study with statistical power is required to quantify trust and diagnostic utility gains.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented a complete, end-to-end dental AI pipeline that distils a 28.51\,M-parameter Swin-T teacher into a 0.118\,M TinyUNet student, achieving 0.8886 Dice on an external TUFTS holdout, a macro-F1 of 0.7284 across four pathologies with zero missed deep caries, and real-time inference at 147.1\,FPS---all trained in 3.55 hours on a laptop GPU. By coupling this with Grad-CAM, LIME, and KernelSHAP explainability and Monte Carlo Dropout epistemic triage (82.2\% auto-approval), the framework is designed not merely to predict, but to explain and know its limits, making it a practical candidate for chairside deployment in resource-limited dental clinics.
